1. The big picture

Two kinds of systems: one to run the business, one to watch it.

Operational (OLTP)Data warehouse (analytics)
JobRun the business (ring up a sale)Watch the business (spot trends)
TouchesOne row at a timeMillions of rows at once
QueriesPredictable, repetitiveAd-hoc, always changing
ScopeOne business unitThe whole enterprise
Time horizonNowTrends over months/years
Design goalFast writes, no duplication (normalized)Fast reads, easy to understand (denormalized)

Kimball's First Law: a database copy of your operational system is not a data warehouse — different users + different needs mean the design must be different. And DW = BI = DSS are just renamed eras of the same idea (Decision Support System → Data Warehousing → Business Intelligence).

LAYMAN

The checkout till is great at ringing up your basket but useless for "which store sold the most ice cream last summer?" The manager's analytics dashboard is the opposite. Same numbers, totally different job — so they need different designs.

The filing-cabinet story from the talk: an insurance company keeps every policy on paper, filed by surname. "What's my policy number?" → easy. "What's our total exposure by region?" → technically possible, but you'd have to pull every file out — a 3-month answer to a question the boss wants now. That gap is the OLTP-vs-warehouse difference.

REMEMBER

OLTP = Write-optimized to Run  |  DW = Read-optimized to Report. First Law: "a copy of the operational DB is NOT a warehouse."

2. The Six Universal Requirements

Kimball found nearly every organisation wants the same six things from a warehouse:

  1. Accessibility — not just available, but understandable, intuitively named in business terms, and fast.
  2. Consistency — one enterprise-wide definition of customer, product, etc.
  3. Adaptability — decisions change by nature, so the DW will change; build it resilient.
  4. Security — aggregated data is sensitive even when single rows aren't.
  5. Decision support — the real output is the decisions made afterwards.
  6. Acceptance — if users won't use it, it failed (rule of thumb: dead within ~6 months = failure).

LAYMAN

  • Accessibility: handing a user 400 cryptic tables (CUST_MSTR_TBL_02) is like giving someone a library with no signs and books in a language they don't read. Available ≠ usable.
  • Consistency: Sales says "we sold 10,000 units," Delivery says "we shipped 11,000" — because one counts by billing address and the other by delivery address. Two "truths" drive managers nuts.
  • Adaptability: the questions people ask always change — build it like LEGO you can rearrange, not a concrete wall.
  • Security: your payslip isn't secret to you, but hand someone everyone's payslips and they can predict the whole company's payroll. In the talk: a full General Ledger feed let people predict company profit before it hit the stock market.
  • Decision support: a beautiful dashboard nobody acts on is a screensaver. The point is better decisions.
  • Acceptance: people must use the checkout, but nobody has to use a report — build a bad one and they'll just guess on gut instead.

REMEMBER

Mnemonic "A-C-A-S-D-A" → Accessible, Consistent, Adaptable, Secure, Decision-support, Accepted. (Two A's bookend it.)

3. Structure — The Restaurant Analogy

Kimball's favourite mental model for a warehouse's three parts:

Suppliers

Source systems deliver raw ingredients (raw data).

Kitchen

Staging area turns raw ingredients into finished meals (ETL).

Dining room

Presentation area where users are served polished data.

LAYMAN

Diners never walk into the kitchen — it's full of sharp knives and hot pans (messy raw data, half-finished transformations). And waiters don't cook. In the same way, business users only ever see the tidy "dining room" (reports/dashboards); they never touch the raw feeds or the ETL mess behind the swinging doors.

REMEMBER

Sources → Staging → Presentation = Suppliers → Kitchen → Dining room. Users only ever see the dining room.

4. Staging Area & ETL (Extract → Transform → Load)

The staging area = everything between acquiring source data and presenting it: raw acquired data, the ETL software, and any storage not shown to users.

  • Extract — keep it dead simple (SELECT * FROM table). Source systems don't care about you; do zero logic here or you'll be blamed when their nightly batch runs late.
  • Transform — do all the work here: cleansing (e.g. address parsing), combining sources, de-duplication, and assigning surrogate ("warehouse") keys — your own numeric IDs you never trust the source to keep stable.
  • Load — bulk overnight (drop indexes → parallel load → rebuild → notify users) or real-time (mimic the source: commit transactionally-consistent units, use parallelism, exploit the 24-hr window vs a tight overnight window).

LAYMAN

  • Extract simply: when you collect groceries from the shop, just take the bags home — don't start chopping onions in the car park. Chop (transform) in your own kitchen.
  • Surrogate keys: like giving every new employee an internal staff number instead of relying on their phone number — because phone numbers change, get reused, and aren't yours to control.
  • Real-time consistency: serve a burger with its bun and fries together — never send out half an order. If you load an invoice header without its line items, users briefly see nonsense ("putting Humpty back together").

REMEMBER

Extract simply, Transform in the kitchen, Load in bulk or real-time. Never transform during extract. Always mint your own surrogate keys.

5. Inmon vs Kimball — should staging be normalized?

  • Bill Inmon (Kimball's "mortal enemy"): build one big normalized enterprise model first.
  • Kimball (v1): go straight to dimensional — no normalized layer.
  • Popular hybrid: acquire → hidden normalized layer → dimensional models for users.
  • Kimball's minimum bar: persist raw data in a readily accessible format (flat file, XML, or DB tables) and never delete it. Ross prefers relational tables (most recoverable), and endorses multiple persistence points so you can fix transformation errors without re-hitting the source.

LAYMAN

Think of keeping your receipts. Kimball just says: keep them somewhere you can actually re-read them later (a labelled shoebox), and never throw them out — so if you later mis-categorise a purchase you can redo it from the receipt without going back to the shop. Inmon says: type every receipt into one giant master ledger first. The common compromise: keep the shoebox and a tidy ledger — but only ever show guests the final summary.

REMEMBER

Inmon = top-down normalized enterprise DW (then marts). Kimball = bottom-up dimensional marts (bus of conformed dims). Most shops run a hybrid. Always keep the raw data.

6. Presentation Area & Data Marts

  • Users see only dimensional data — never the kitchen.
  • "Data mart" is overloaded: it can mean a dependent side-system, a mini-DW for one unit, or (Kimball's usage) essentially a star schema (one fact + its dimensions).
  • Organise by business process, not department (processes cross departments): sales, deliveries, fulfillment, commissions, profitability.

LAYMAN

A data mart is a themed buffet station: one for "sales," one for "deliveries." A department is who cooks; a business process is the meal being served. "Fulfillment" needs ingredients from both the orders team and the delivery team — so you organise the station around the meal, not the kitchen staff.

REMEMBER

Model by business process, not department. Conformed dimensions are the "bus" that connects all the marts so you can drill across them.

7. The star schema + the Three Axioms

From the CEO's throwaway line: "We sell products in various markets and measure our performance over time." That gives you a cube — facts in the cells, dimensions on the axes. Physically it becomes a star schema: one central fact table (the numbers) surrounded by dimension tables (the descriptions).

Kimball's Three Axioms of the presentation layer:

  1. Dimensionally modelled — facts + dimensions, not normalized tables.
  2. Detailed atomic facts — keep the lowest grain; build aggregates only for speed.
  3. Conformed dimensions & facts — same name ⇒ same meaning; same meaning ⇒ same name.

Anatomy recap. A fact table holds only: a surrogate primary key (warehouse-assigned, never shown on screen), foreign keys to each dimension, and measures (numeric, usually additive) — plus rare exceptions (degenerate dims, drill-through attrs). A dimension table holds: a surrogate key, the source natural key, and lots of descriptive/hierarchical attributes for roll-up & drill-down.

The Big Four dimensions to hunt for on day one: Customer, Location (store), Product, Time (date).

Golden rule — NEVER snowflake. Don't split brand and category into their own sub-tables. It looks "tidier" (your OLTP training screaming) but it hurts the two things a star exists for: performance and understandability. Denormalize deliberately.

LAYMAN

Cube: picture a Rubik's cube — one axis products, one stores, one months; each cell holds one answer ("bananas, store 5, March = $200"). Fact vs dimension: the fact table is the till roll (long, skinny "what happened"); dimensions are the reference booklets (short but packed with detail). Snowflaking is a recipe that says "for flour see Booklet 3; for sugar see Booklet 7" — a star prints it all on one page.

REMEMBER

Fact = skinny & tall (keys + measures). Dimension = fat & short. Star good, snowflake bad. Axioms = "Dimensional, Atomic, Conformed" (D-A-C). Atomic facts enable drill-through; conformed dims enable drill-across.

8. FACTS — every type in one place

A fact = one row per measurement event, at a fixed grain. There are four flavours:

8a. Transaction fact

One row per discrete event (a sale scan). The bread-and-butter fact. Fully additive; insert-only.

8b. Periodic snapshot fact

One row per entity per time period — measures the state at intervals, even when nothing "happened."

SAMPLE DATA — month-end loyalty points balance

customer_keymonthpoints_balance
12012024-01500
12012024-02650
12012024-03400

You can't SUM these balances across months (500+650+400 = meaningless). See "Additivity" below.

8c. Accumulating snapshot fact

One row per process instance with a lifecycle — you update the row as milestones happen. Lots of date columns.

SAMPLE DATA — online grocery order lifecycle

order_noordered_datepicked_dateshipped_datedelivered_dateorder_total
ORD-9002024-03-012024-03-012024-03-02(null)84.20

Same row gets updated when it's delivered — then you can measure "days from order to doorstep." You can also build a separate transaction fact for each state-change event — two facts, same process, different questions.

8d. Factless / coverage fact

Just dimension keys, no measures — records that a relationship existed, so you can report on what didn't happen.

WHY THEY'RE NEEDED

The other three fact types (Transaction, Periodic, Accumulating) all record things that happened — a sale, a balance, a shipment. But a huge class of business questions is about the absence of an event:

  • Which products were on the shelf but never sold?
  • Which vehicles sat in the depot and were never rented?
  • Which customers were eligible for a promotion but didn't redeem it?
  • Which students were enrolled in a class but didn't attend?

You cannot answer these from an events fact alone — if nothing happened, there's simply no row to query. The fix is to record the full universe of possibilities (the "coverage") in a fact that has only dimension keys and no measures. Then "what didn't happen" = (everything that was possible) − (everything that happened). That subtraction is the whole point.

Two common shapes: (1) coverage / eligibility facts (what could have happened), and (2) event factless facts where the event itself has no number (e.g. "student attended class" — there's nothing to measure, you just count rows).

SAMPLE DATA (retail) — which products were on-shelf vs actually sold

Bananas & milk appear in FACT_SALES; kale doesn't → kale was available but never sold that day. Impossible to spot without a factless fact.

SAMPLE DATA (vehicle rentals — the classic Kimball example)

A car-rental company has a normal rental transaction fact — but it only records vehicles that were rented. Ask "how many vehicles sat idle at Depot 9 on 22 Mar?" and it can't answer — idle cars generate no rows. So you add a vehicle-availability factless fact: one row per vehicle, per day, per depot that the car was available — no measures, just the fact that it could have been rented.

On 22 Mar at Depot 9, four cars were available (V-1..V-4) but only three were rented (V-1, V-2, V-4). Subtract the rentals from the availability → V-3 was idle all day (lost revenue!). LAYMAN

  • Transaction = individual till receipts.
  • Periodic snapshot = your monthly bank statement balance.
  • Accumulating snapshot = a parcel-tracking page you refresh as it moves.
  • Factless = a class attendance sheet, or a rental depot's "cars on the lot today" list — you spot the no-shows / idle cars by seeing who's on the list but missing from the action.

REMEMBER

Fact types = "TPAF" → Transaction, Periodic snapshot, Accumulating snapshot, Factless. Trick: Transaction inserts, Accumulating updates, Periodic repeats, Factless has no number.

8e. Additivity (how you're allowed to SUM)

TypeMeaningRetail example
AdditiveSum across ALL dimensionsSales amount, units sold
Semi-additiveSum across some, NOT across timeLoyalty balance, stock-on-hand (use avg/last over time)
Non-additiveCan't sum at allUnit price, margin % (average or recompute instead)

REMEMBER

"Amounts & counts = full; balances & levels = semi; ratios & prices = non." If it's a snapshot balance, never SUM it over time — average or take last.

8f. Degenerate dimensions, drill-through bloat & master-detail

A degenerate dimension is a dimension attribute that has no attributes of its own, so it doesn't earn a separate dimension table — it just sits inside the fact table. The classic is the order/invoice/receipt number. After you've pushed product, customer, store and date out into their own dimensions, that order number is left over — there's nothing else to describe about it (no "order colour" or "order category"), so building a one-column DIM_ORDER would be pointless. It stays in the fact as a plain column. The name is a joke: it's a "dimension" that has degenerated down to just its key.

SAMPLE DATA — order_no living inside FACT_SALES as a degenerate dimension

sale_keyorder_no (degenerate dim)date_keyproduct_keycustomer_keyqtyamount
1001ORD-9002024031577 (bananas)120127.00
1002ORD-9002024031581 (milk)120113.50
1003ORD-9012024031577 (bananas)1450621.00

No DIM_ORDER table exists — order_no is just a column. Note ORD-900 spans two rows (two items in one basket).

What it's actually used for:

  • Drill-through (the big one): you roll up to "Produce sales in March = $50,000," then a manager asks "show me the actual transactions behind that." You drill down to individual fact rows — and the order_no is what lets you display and group those rows back into real baskets/receipts a human recognises. Without it, the atomic rows are anonymous.
  • Count-distinct: "how many orders did we take today?" = COUNT(DISTINCT order_no). The fact has one row per line item, so counting rows would over-count — the degenerate dim gives you the true basket/order count. Same trick for average basket size = total amount ÷ distinct orders.
  • Drill-across / stitching facts: if two facts share the same order_no (e.g. an order-header fact and an order-line fact, or a shipments fact), you can join them on it to follow one order end-to-end.

SAMPLE DATA — drill path using the degenerate dimension

LevelQueryResult
Roll-upSales by category, MarchProduce = $50,000
Drill-down… expand Produce → distinct orders1,200 orders
Drill-through… open order ORD-900Bananas $7.00 + Milk $3.50 = $10.50 basket

Don't fatten facts: extra drill-through-only attributes (client ref, free-text comments, millisecond timestamps) make rows fat, and since a query reads the whole row, fatter rows = slower analytics. Allow one or two, migrate the rest to a side table you only join for the few hundred rows you actually drill into.

Master-detail (order + order-lines): roll everything down to the line grain. An order-level discount can't be repeated on every line (double-count!) — pro-rate it across lines, or keep it in a separate order-level fact and join via the order-number degenerate dimension.

SAMPLE DATA — a $6 basket coupon pro-rated across 3 items

0.60 + 1.20 + 4.20 = $6.00 exactly. Repeating "$6" on all three lines would wrongly total $18.

LAYMAN

The order number is like the reference on your receipt — you'd never chart "sales by receipt number," but it's exactly what you quote to pull up that one transaction, and it's what groups your loose items back into "one shop." And a "$6 off your whole basket" coupon can't be stamped "$6 off" on every item — you split it fairly. Stuffing long comment fields into the main fact is like making everyone lug the entire instruction manual just to read the price tag.

9. DIMENSIONS — every special case in one place

A dimension = the who / what / where / when that describes a fact. Before the special shapes, one base rule matters most: conformed dimensions.

9-base. What "conformed" actually means

A conformed dimension is a single, shared dimension table used identically by more than one fact table. The rule of thumb: same name ⇒ same meaning, and same meaning ⇒ same name. If both your Sales fact and your Returns fact point at the exact same DIM_CUSTOMER and DIM_DATE, those dimensions are "conformed."

Why it matters: conformed dimensions are what let you drill across — combine numbers from two different facts in one report. If Sales used its own "customer" and Returns used a different "customer" definition, you could never trust a report comparing them — apples to oranges. Conformed dimensions are the shared backbone (Kimball calls it the bus) that ties all your separate star schemas into one coherent warehouse.

SAMPLE DATA — one shared DIM_CUSTOMER feeding two facts → drill-across works

DIM_CUSTOMER (shared)nameregion
customer_key 1201KimEast
FACT_SALEScustomer_keysales_amt
row1201500.00
FACT_RETURNScustomer_keyreturn_amt

Both facts use the same customer_key 1201 → you can build one report: "Kim: $500 sales, $40 returns, net $460." That combined view is drill-across, and it's only possible because DIM_CUSTOMER is conformed.

LAYMAN

Conformed = everyone in the company uses the same dictionary. If "Region = South" means the exact same set of stores to the Sales team, the Returns team and Marketing, you can safely put their numbers on one page. If each team quietly has its own idea of "South," every cross-team report is a lie. It's the "a cup means 250ml to everybody" rule.

REMEMBER

Conformed dimension = shared & identical across facts → enables drill-across. Same name = same meaning. It's the "bus" that connects all your star schemas.

Beyond the basics, dimensions come in special shapes:

9a. The Date dimension (you always build one)

Store dates as a table, not raw dates, pre-populated for ~100 years past & future (only a few hundred thousand tiny rows). Why? (1) You can't index every date-function on a billion-row fact — instead filter the small date table then use its keys against one fact index. (2) Things like public holidays or fiscal 4-4-5 weeks can't be derived by any formula.

SAMPLE DATA — DIM_DATE

date_keydateday_namemonthquarteris_weekendis_holidayfiscal_wk
202412252024-12-25WednesdayDecQ4NoYes (Xmas)52
202412262024-12-26ThursdayDecQ4NoYes (Boxing)52

Finest grain = one day (Kimball: finer divisions "aren't analytically interesting"). Handle sub-day needs like this:

  • Exact timestamps → store as a plain attribute on the fact for drill-through display (not for roll-up).
  • Durations (gap between two events) → calculate in ETL, store as an additive measure.
  • Time-of-day banding (peak/off-peak) → a separate time-of-day dimension (8am Monday = 8am Tuesday; it's time-related, not date-related).
  • Globalization (one fact rolls up to different days per time zone) → the genuinely hard one; finer date-dim rows help, but even Kimball hasn't fully cracked per-user roll-up.

LAYMAN

It's a pre-printed wall calendar with notes. The computer can work out "what weekday is 25 Dec" — but only your scribbled note tells it "that's a public holiday." And "what day did it happen?" vs "was it during the morning rush?" are two different questions, so they get two different lookups.

9b. Role-playing, Outrigger & Junk dimensions

Role-playing dimension

What it is: one physical dimension table that gets used several times in the same fact, each time meaning something different (playing a different "role"). The textbook case is DIM_DATE: an order has an order date, a ship date, and a delivery date — all three are just dates, so all three point back to the same DIM_DATE table.

Why needed / the problem it solves: you do not want to build three near-identical date tables (DIM_ORDER_DATE, DIM_SHIP_DATE, DIM_DELIVERY_DATE) — that's wasteful and they'd drift out of sync. But if all three foreign keys point at one table and a user drags in "Month Name," they can't tell which date they got. Fix: keep ONE physical table, and expose it under multiple aliases / views ("Order Date," "Ship Date," "Delivery Date") so reports stay unambiguous.

Outrigger dimension

What it is: a dimension that hangs off another dimension (a dimension pointing to a dimension), instead of hanging off the fact. The allowed case: a dimension has a date inside it — e.g. DIM_CUSTOMER has a date_of_birth — and you want the rich date attributes (birth month, birth decade, day-of-week) for analysis. So DIM_CUSTOMER carries a dob_date_key that points into the shared DIM_DATE. That secondary dimension is the "outrigger."

Why needed: it lets you reuse the powerful date dimension for an attribute buried inside another dimension, without rebuilding all that calendar logic. Isn't this snowflaking? Technically yes (a dimension is now 2 hops from the fact) — but Kimball explicitly blesses it as an outrigger because you're reusing an existing standalone dimension, not normalizing for tidiness. Caveat: use sparingly — if you find yourself outrigging all over the place, you've over-normalized your dimensions.

SAMPLE DATA — DIM_CUSTOMER outrigged to DIM_DATE via date-of-birth

DIM_CUSTOMERnameregiondob_date_key → DIM_DATE
customer_key 1201KimEast19900412

Follow 19900412 into DIM_DATE and you instantly get birth-month, birth-decade, birthday day-of-week — reused, not rebuilt. Now you can analyze "sales by customer's birth decade" for free.

Junk dimension

What it is: after you've moved product/customer/store/date out to real dimensions, the fact is often left with a handful of low-cardinality flags & indicators — gift-wrapped? gift receipt? loyalty used? — that don't belong to any real dimension and aren't related to each other. A junk dimension sweeps all of them into one small table (one row per combination of flag values) and the fact carries a single foreign key to it.

Why needed / the problem it solves: the alternatives are ugly. Leaving 4 flags in the fact bloats it; making 4 separate tiny dimensions means 4 extra joins in every query. A junk dimension = one extra key and one join gives you all the flags. Caveat: junk dimensions are not conformed — the flag bundle on the Sales fact differs from the bundle on the Promotions fact — so you can't drill across on a junk attribute (usually you don't want to anyway).

LAYMAN

  • Role-playing = the same actor wearing different costumes. One calendar on the wall, but you label three windows onto it: "ordered," "shipped," "delivered" — so nobody mixes them up. You don't buy three calendars.
  • Outrigger = you already own one really good wall calendar; when a customer file mentions a birthday, you just point at that same calendar instead of building a new one.
  • Junk dimension = the kitchen junk drawer: batteries, rubber bands, twist ties — unrelated bits that each don't deserve their own labelled cabinet, so you toss them in one drawer and grab the whole drawer with one pull.

9c. Monster & Mini dimensions

The pain point: a monster dimension is huge in both directions — millions of customers × hundreds of attributes. Some of those attributes (age band, income, marital status) change fairly often. If you make the whole thing Type 2 (new row on every change), it explodes: 5M customers × ~10 attribute changes/year = 50M new rows/year → 250M rows in 5 years. Joining that to a billion-row fact = terrible performance.

The fix: keep the customer dimension itself Type 1 (small, stable), and lift the volatile attributes out into a separate small mini dimension that holds one row per unique combination of those attribute values (no customer id at all). The fact stores the mini-dimension key that applied at the time of the event — so the customer's history lives in the trail of keys across their fact rows, not in a bloated dimension.

SAMPLE DATA — DIM_CUSTOMER_DEMOGRAPHICS (mini; note: NO customer id!)

demo_keyage_bandincome_bandhas_kids
D118-25LowNo
D226-35MediumNo
D336-45MediumYes

One row (D1) covers ALL "18-25 / Low / No kids" customers — maybe 500,000 people share it. Max size = age_bands × income_bands × kids = tiny (here just a few hundred rows), no matter how many millions of customers you have.

SAMPLE DATA — the history lives in the FACT, via the demo_key

DRAWBACKS & GOTCHAS of mini dimensions

  • The mini dimension itself is SCD Type 1 — it is NOT versioned. When Kim ages into a new band you do not update or end-date any mini row; you simply point her next fact at a different existing mini row (or add a brand-new combination if it's never been seen). The mini table just holds all possible combinations.
  • You lose point-in-time profile history OUTSIDE of a fact. If a customer hasn't transacted since their profile changed, there's no fact row to carry the new demo_key — so you can't tell "what was Kim's income band on 1 Jan" unless a fact happened around then. History is only as granular as the events.
  • Banding loses precision. You bucket age into "18-25" etc. to keep combinations small — so you can't query exact age, only the band. Choosing bands is a design trade-off (too fine → the mini table grows toward monster size again).
  • Correlated changes look like one jump. If several attributes change between two purchases, you only see the before/after snapshot on those two facts, not the intermediate steps.
  • Analyzing the customer dimension alone doesn't show demographics — because you moved them out. That's exactly what Type 5 fixes (put the current demo_key back into DIM_CUSTOMER — see SCD section).

LAYMAN

Instead of a fresh profile photocopy every time any tiny detail changes (cabinet explodes!), you make a few reusable "shopper-type stickers" ("young-single-low-income"). Millions of people share the same few stickers; each purchase notes which sticker fit them that day. The catch: the stickers themselves never change, and you only learn someone switched stickers when they next shop — if they go quiet, you're in the dark until they return.

9d. Bridge tables (many-to-many)

The pain point: a normal star assumes one fact row → one dimension row (many-to-one). But real life has many-to-many: one sale can be driven by several promotions; one hospital treatment can address several diagnoses; one insurance policy can have several holders. You can't just put a single promo_key on the fact — which one would you pick?

The naive fix (don't): a raw cross-reference table joining the million-row fact directly to a million-row promotion list. Joining two enormous tables kills performance — "that dog won't hunt."

The Kimball fix: introduce a small reusable group dimension (one row per combination that actually occurs), put a single group key on the fact, and use a tiny bridge to resolve the group into its individual members. Then guard against double-counting with a weighting factor (pro-rate the measure) or a primary flag (count only the main member).

One doctor's visit, several things wrong with you (say a $250 visit treating both osteoporosis and a fractured leg). If you naively bill "per diagnosis" you charge $500 for one visit. So you either split the bill across the diagnoses (weighting) or count only the main reason for the visit (primary flag). The "group" is like a combo meal — lots of patients have the exact same set of ailments, so you reuse the same group instead of re-listing every pairing.

9e. Ragged (variable-depth) hierarchies

The pain point: normal hierarchies have neat, fixed, labelled levels (Day → Month → Quarter → Year). A ragged hierarchy has unknown, uneven depth. Example: BargainMart's business customers — "ACME Store" belongs to "ACME Region," which belongs to "ACME Holdings"... and some chains are 3 levels deep, others 8. You can't ask for "level-3 customers" (a level may not even exist on one branch); you ask "show all sales for ACME Holdings and everything under it."

The naive fix (don't): a self-referencing parent_key column. Easy to store, but a nightmare to query — you don't know how many self-joins to write (3? 8?), and recursive SQL (CONNECT BY) is slow on big tables.

The Kimball fix: pre-explode the tree into a hierarchy bridge listing every ancestor–descendant pair (including each node paired with itself). Then "everything under X" is a single lookup at any depth.

GOTCHAS

  • Double-counting: selecting two ancestors where one is inside the other double-counts the overlap. Stay safe by filtering to a single ancestor, or to leaf/same-level nodes (use the is_leaf / levels_apart helper columns).
  • Size is bounded: rows ≈ nodes × average depth (e.g. 5M customers × ~10 = 50M) — big but capped; it won't keep growing like a Type-2 monster.
  • Some BI tools (SSAS) and Oracle CONNECT BY handle ragged trees for you — but the pre-built bridge usually performs better.

LAYMAN

A family tree / company org-chart of unknown depth. Rather than climbing branch by branch every time someone asks "show me everyone under Grandma," you pre-write a cheat-sheet pairing every person with each of their ancestors ("Tim→Dad, Tim→Grandma, Tim→Great-Grandma"). Now "everyone under Grandma" is a single lookup instead of a scavenger hunt.

REMEMBER

Special dimensions hooks: Conformed, Role-playing, Outrigger, Date, Junk, Mini/monster, Bridge, Ragged. Many-to-many → Bridge (weight or primary-flag to avoid double-count). Variable depth → Ragged / pair-bridge. Huge, fast-changing dimension → Mini.

10. Slowly Changing Dimensions (SCD) — Type 0 to 6

What do you do when a dimension attribute changes? This is the #1 interview topic. The choice is made per COLUMN, not per table.

Scenario: customer Kim (customer #C-42) moves from the North region to East, and later changes her surname.

Type 0 — frozen (never changes)

e.g. original signup date. Ignore updates.

Type 1 — overwrite (no history)

cust_keycust_idnameregion
7C-42KimEast

Region just gets overwritten North→East. Old sales now wrongly look like they were in East. Fine only if you don't care about history.

Type 2 — add a new row (full history) — the workhorse

cust_keycust_idnameregioneffectiveexpirycurrent?
7C-42KimNorth2022-04-122023-05-31N
8C-42KimEast2023-05-319999-12-31Y

New surrogate key (8), same cust_id (C-42). Old sales join to key 7 (North); new sales to key 8 (East). Keep a contiguous, gap-free timeline; make the expiry date non-inclusive so a sale exactly on the boundary lands cleanly on the new row.

Current-flag vs effective/expiry dates: a simple "current row" flag breaks for a late-arriving sale (a file from last week would wrongly attach to today's row). Effective/expiry dates fix this — compare the sale's date to each row's range and pick the row active at the time of the sale.

LAYMAN — business date, not system date (the #1 mistake)

Which date goes in effective/expiry? The business date (when the change was actually effective), NOT the system/ETL date (when the warehouse happened to load it). Example: "Joe is promoted and starts earning commission immediately" — his commission starts on his promotion day, not on data-entry day. Sometimes system date is the business date (e.g. e-commerce where the customer updates their own details live). Precision: make dates as accurate as the business date genuinely is (often just day-level; sometimes only month or financial year) — don't invent second-level precision that doesn't exist. When granularity is coarse and many updates share a period, just keep updating the current row instead of spawning duplicate same-dated rows.

Type 3 — add a "prior value" column (limited history)

cust_keycust_idcurrent_regionprior_region
7C-42EastNorth

One row keeps both "now" and "before." Great for a one-off reorg (run reports the old way or the new way for a few weeks). Useless if it keeps changing. NOT "better than Type 2" — different job.

Type 4 — mini dimension

Move fast-changing attributes to a small mini dimension (see 9c). Base dimension stays Type 1. History is tracked by which mini-row the fact points at.

Type 5 — Type 4 + mini keys back in base (current view)

Add the mini-dimension's current key into the customer dimension itself so you can analyze customers "as they are now" ("how many under-25, low-income, no-kids customers in the north right now?") without touching a fact table. The base dim stays Type 1; this added key is kept current (overwritten) as demographics change. You can't ask about last year from here — that history still lives in the fact via Type 4.

Type 6 — hybrid (1 + 2 + 3)

Different columns behave differently in one table (the SCD type is chosen per-column). Here: region is Type 2 (new row, keep history), a name-spelling fix is Type 1 (overwrite everywhere so Kim isn't split into two people in a "top customers" report), and a current_region column is Type 3 (always shows today's value on every historical row). The name "Type 6" comes from 1 + 2 + 3 = 6.

Your phone contacts: if a friend moves house you want their address history (Type 2). If you just fix a typo in their name, you overwrite it everywhere so it's not a second contact (Type 1). Type 3 = a form with "previous address" and "current address" boxes.

11. The 4-step design process

  1. Pick the business process — what decision matters? Ask the CEO/senior manager what questions they ask (process, not department). e.g. retail sales.
  2. Declare the grain — what is one row? (one product on one receipt line) — and stick to it.
  3. Choose the dimensions — date, product, store, customer, promotion.
  4. Identify the facts — qty, sales amount, cost, discount.

Always acquire the atomic grain first; build aggregate star schemas on top only for performance. Do all enrichment/business rules in staging, never in the presentation layer. Keep measures from different measurement processes in separate facts.

LAYMAN

Record one row per item on the receipt, not per whole receipt — you can always add items up to a total, but you can never split a total back into items. Grab the finest useful detail first.

REMEMBER

Order matters: Process → Grain → Dimensions → Facts. Mnemonic: "Please Grab Dimensional Facts." Everything serves two north stars: simplicity & performance.

12. Bonus gotchas worth knowing

  • Surrogate vs natural key: always mint your own numeric warehouse key (surrogate); keep the source's key (SKU, cust_id) as the natural key. Never trust source keys to stay stable, and never display the surrogate on screen.
  • Business date, not system date: the #1 SCD mistake (see SCD Type 2 above).
  • Extract simply, transform in staging: pull raw data (SELECT *), do all logic in the "kitchen," never in the presentation layer.
  • Keep facts skinny: don't bloat fact rows with drill-through junk (long comments) — fatter rows = slower scans. Migrate them to a side table.
  • Hard deletes at source: use change-data-capture, or compare full/scoped extracts to detect what vanished. It's a business problem too — the source must come to the party.
  • Multiple facts per process: totally normal — e.g. an accumulating snapshot for the whole order plus a transaction fact for each status change.
  • Persist & never delete raw data: keep the "shoebox of receipts" so you can re-run transforms without re-querying the source.

Rapid-fire Q&A (say these out loud):

Q: OLTP vs data warehouse?
OLTP runs the business (write-optimized, normalized, one row at a time). DW watches the business (read-optimized, denormalized, millions of rows). A copy of OLTP is NOT a warehouse.
Q: Star vs snowflake?
Star = denormalized dims (fast, simple). Snowflake = normalized dims (avoid; hurts performance & clarity).
Q: Fact vs dimension?
Fact = the measurements (skinny, tall, additive numbers). Dimension = the descriptions (fat, short, textual).
Q: What's grain?
The meaning of one fact row. Declare it early, keep it consistent, go as atomic as possible.
Q: The 4 design steps?
Process → Grain → Dimensions → Facts.
Q: Inmon vs Kimball?
Inmon = top-down normalized enterprise DW then marts. Kimball = bottom-up conformed dimensional marts. Most shops hybridize.
Q: Difference between SCD Type 1, 2, 3?
1 = overwrite (no history); 2 = new row (full history, the default); 3 = new column (one prior value).
Q: Type 4/5/6?
4 = mini dimension for volatile attributes; 5 = 4 + current mini-key back in base; 6 = hybrid 1+2+3 (per-column behaviour).
Q: Business date vs system date in SCD Type 2?
Always use the business (effective) date, not the load date. It's the most common mistake.
Q: How to handle a huge, fast-changing customer dimension?
Keep it Type 1; move volatile attributes into a mini dimension (Type 4); optionally add the current mini-key back (Type 5).
Q: Many-to-many between fact and dimension?
Use a bridge table (with a group dimension); guard against double-counting with weighting factors or a primary flag.
Q: How to report on what DIDN'T happen (e.g. unsold products)?
A factless / coverage fact (dimension keys, no measures).
Q: Semi-additive fact example?
Account/loyalty balance or stock-on-hand — sum across product/store, but NOT across time (use average or last).
Q: Degenerate dimension?
A dimension attribute (like order number) with no attributes of its own, so it lives in the fact table.
Q: Conformed dimension?
A shared, identical dimension across facts — enables drill-across (compare sales vs returns for the same customer).
Q: Role-playing dimension?
One physical dimension used in multiple roles via aliases/views (order date vs ship date).
Q: Why a date dimension instead of raw dates?
You can't index every date function, and non-derivable attributes (holidays, fiscal weeks) need somewhere to live.
